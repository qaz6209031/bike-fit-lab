    How to film for accurate results
    1. Put the bike on an indoor trainer and level it.
    2. Place the camera square to your side, at hip height, 2.5–4 m away, in landscape. Your whole body and both wheels must be in frame.
    3. Wear close-fitting clothes. Use even light and a plain background.
    4. Hold the hoods. After warming up, pedal steadily at about 80–90 rpm. The test stops by itself once it has 20 clean pedal strokes, usually 15–20 seconds.
    5. The side nearest the camera is analyzed. If you know you are asymmetric, film each side.
    6. Before starting, press “Calibrate scale” and click the front and rear edges of the front tire. This converts to real measurements and corrects camera tilt.
    7. Using a computer? For the best accuracy, record on your phone and upload the video here. On iPhone, set Settings › Camera › Formats to Most Compatible so the video opens on any computer. A laptop webcam also works if it can sit at hip height, 2.5–4 m to your side.

    How it works

    A fitter watches you pedal, measures a few angles and compares them with what works for most riders. This site does the same steps with your phone camera.

    1. Film from the side

      You pedal on a trainer while the camera sees your whole body side-on. A live camera or an uploaded video both work.

    2. Find your joints

      Google MediaPipe, a pose detection model running in your browser, marks your shoulder, elbow, wrist, hip, knee, ankle and foot in every frame. The video never leaves your device.

    3. Measure every pedal stroke

      From those points it measures your knee, hip, back, shoulder and elbow angles, and follows your foot around the pedal circle to find the crank and bottom bracket. It keeps going until it has 20 full strokes.

    4. Use your typical stroke, not one frame

      Your angles change a little every stroke, so it uses where your knee and hip usually end up at the top and bottom of the stroke. One sloppy stroke does not skew the result.

    5. Compare and suggest

      Each angle is checked against common bike fit ranges for your riding style, for example a knee angle of 140–150° at the bottom of the stroke. Anything outside a range becomes an adjustment, biggest first, with an estimated amount in millimeters.

    Because it uses one camera from the side, it measures what is visible from the side. It cannot see cleat rotation or how your knees move side to side.

    Why I built this

    A professional bike fit cost me about $400–500, and I have had one at two different shops. Their advice did not match. One fitter said my torso was short, so I should not ride an aggressive position. The other said my body could go very aggressive.

    I also went back to the same fitter for a second session. The first time, he told me to get an 80 mm stem, so I ordered one and installed it. The second time, he said my original stem suited me better. With internal cable routing, swapping a stem on my bike is not a quick job.

    That is when I realized that even an expensive bike fit is partly opinion, and different fitters give different answers. What they measure is mostly a few joint angles compared with standard ranges, and a phone camera with an open-source pose model from Google can do that. So this site does it for free: no account, no upload, no app to install. Get the basics dialed in yourself and spend the money on bike gear instead.
